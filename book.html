As AI takes over the work, influence is what's left

AI can summarize your meeting, draft your email and organize your information. Every professional now has that. What still separates the people who get promoted is everything AI can't do for you.

What AI does well

  • Summarizes the meeting
  • Drafts the email
  • Organizes the information

What only you can do

  • Read the room
  • Recognize resistance
  • Build trust
  • Know when to push, pause or change your approach

Those skills are becoming more valuable, not less. This book is about building them, and about using AI to build them faster.

The AI coaching system

Most leadership books end where the hard part begins. The workbook's "AI + You" sections show you how to use ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot for the part AI does well, and where you still have to do the thinking.

"AI is strong at compressing a rambling draft into a headline-first message. It is not good at knowing which specific fact your director cares about, because it doesn't know your director."

The Two-Pass Method

Pass 1: AI

Paste your rough draft into AI. Ask for a headline-first rewrite.

Pass 2: You

Delete one sentence that sounds generic. Replace it with a specific detail only you know about this stakeholder.

Send

Your message now has AI's clarity and your specificity.

From the workbook: an AI + You prompt

Here's a draft email I need to send. Rewrite it using the Executive Translation Headline-First Framework. The ask is [what you need]. The deadline is [when]. The reason is [why it matters]. Then give me a version for a peer and a version for a director. Flag anything in your rewrite that sounds generic enough that I should personalize it further.

Your job after the prompt: find one sentence that sounds like anyone could have written it, and replace it with a sentence only you could write.

The workbook is just as clear about where AI stops: "AI can help you find the right words. It cannot tell you whether you delivered them with conviction. For that, you need a real person and a real room."

For international professionals and global teams

Leading across languages, cultures and time zones

Most influence advice assumes one culture. The workbook doesn't. Its scripts are built on the direct, assertive norms common in many Western workplaces, which can backfire where communication is indirect, hierarchical or consensus-driven. So a full section shows you how to adapt them.

The Global Adaptation Checklist

Run every important message through six questions first:

  • How direct should I be?
  • What's the hierarchy?
  • How is the decision made?
  • What's the relationship?
  • How is conflict handled?
  • Is there a language barrier?

Plus the Cultural Influence Spectrum, to see where you and your stakeholders fall.

The same script, adapted

Direct

"I see it differently. Here's why..."

Adapted for a face-saving environment

"That's a perspective I hadn't fully considered. I see the logic in your position. To build on your thinking, I wonder if we might also consider the implications for [risk area]..."

Direct

"I recommend we take Option A. What do you think?"

Adapted for a hierarchical environment

"I have been analyzing the options and believe Option A has the strongest business case. Before I formalize the recommendation, I would value your perspective on the key trade-offs..."

"The goal is not to write a different message for every person. It is to know which part of your message is non-negotiable (the ask, the deadline, the reason) and which part is adjustable (the tone, the framing, the order)."

Also inside: a workplace story about a US project manager leading a joint initiative abroad, who stopped pushing for decisions in meetings and learned to pre-wire them in private conversations first.

Executive presence in the virtual office

"Influence in person is built through 50 small interactions: a hallway check-in, a quick coffee, a follow-up question after a meeting. In remote work, those moments don't exist. You have to create them deliberately."

The same three signals, new channels

The workbook's three signals of executive presence, certainty, completion and structure, don't change when your team is remote. What changes is how you deliver them: in Slack messages, virtual meetings and weekly updates, deliberately and consistently.

From the workbook: the weekly update

  • What I delivered: [specific result]
  • What I'm working on: [current priority]
  • What I need from you: [specific ask + deadline]

No surprises. Just updates.

Plus scripts for flagging a risk on a video call, finishing your point when you're interrupted, and drawing out a silent virtual meeting.
