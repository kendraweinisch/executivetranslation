Executive Presence When English Is Your Second Language

Fluent international leaders often get read as more junior than they are. The cause is rarely accent or grammar. It is structure, and structure can be learned.

By Kendra Weinisch · November 23, 2026

"In meetings with US leadership, the moment passes before I've found the words."

International engineers and tech leaders say some version of this all the time. They're fluent, experienced and very good at their jobs. And they're convinced the problem is their English.

It almost never is. Not accent. Not grammar. Structure and story.

Presence is precision, not volume

You can be the most prepared person in the meeting and still get talked over. That isn't about competence. It's about presence, and presence is a set of learnable habits, not a personality trait.

When you speak, people are silently asking four questions: Does this person understand the situation? Can I trust their judgment? Will following their recommendation make my job easier? Do I need to pay attention? They answer within seconds. Clear, concise and confident communication turns those answers into "yes."

My book describes three signals people read: certainty, completion and structure. None of them requires perfect English. All three can be lost in perfect English.

1. Drop the apology opener

Many international leaders open with an apology for their English. It's meant as politeness. It lands as uncertainty, before you've said a single idea, and it's usually not even true.

Before

"Sorry, my English is not perfect, but I will try to explain."

After

"Let me give you the short answer first."

The second sentence tells the room you have an answer, promises brevity, and puts you in charge of the next 30 seconds.

2. Cut the hedges

Hedging words ("I think," "maybe," "just," "sorry") feel polite, especially if your first language uses more softeners than English does. In a US meeting they read as doubt.

Before

"I think maybe we should consider pushing the deadline?"

After

"I recommend we move the deadline to the 22nd. Here’s why."

Before

"Sorry, can I just say something real quick?"

After

"One point before we decide."

3. Answer first, then two reasons

The most common pattern is building up to the point: context, background, concerns, and finally the recommendation. By then the moment has passed.

Before

"So, we’ve been looking at the vendor options, and there are some concerns about cost and timing, and I think maybe we should consider..."

After

"My recommendation is vendor B. Two reasons: it’s cheaper over three years, and it ships a month earlier."

The answer first. Then two reasons. Context only if they ask. That structure gives you the floor before the moment passes, in any language.

4. Use a pitch, not a biography

"Tell me about yourself" is where many senior engineers lose an interview in the first two minutes, because they answer in chronological order. Interviewers at the senior level want a pitch. Use four parts:

  1. Now: your scope today, in one sentence.
  2. Proof: one decision and what it changed.
  3. Past: one sentence, only what supports the proof.
  4. Next: the scope you want.

Present, proof, past, next. It works in any language, and it is much easier to deliver under pressure than a life story.

5. Finish your sentence when you're interrupted

Being talked over is more common when you pause to find a word. Keep one line ready:

"Let me finish this point, then I want to hear your perspective."

If it keeps happening with the same person, ask privately: "I notice I get cut off in meetings. Is there something about how I'm presenting that's making it hard to get my point across?"

6. Know when direct isn't the right style

The advice above follows US business norms. If you also work with colleagues from indirect, hierarchical or consensus-driven cultures, the same directness can backfire. The skill is knowing which parts of a message are fixed (the ask, the deadline, the reason) and which can flex (the tone, the framing, the order). There's more on this in how to push back on a director.

7. Build presence in writing

Most of your presence is not in the room. It is in your outbox, especially on remote and global teams where there are no hallway conversations. Writing also gives you something speaking doesn't: time. A weekly update with three lines does a lot of work:

  • What I delivered: [specific result]
  • What I'm working on: [current priority]
  • What I need from you: [specific ask and deadline]

Use clear, simple language. Avoid idioms, jokes and cultural references, which travel badly in any direction.

The real gap

If you've been told to "work on your communication," it's tempting to buy an accent course or a business English program. For most senior international professionals, that's solving the wrong problem. Your English is good enough to do the job. What's missing is the structure that makes senior people hear you at the level you work.
